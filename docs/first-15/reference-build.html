Learning Experience Design  ·  Scenario-based onboarding

The first fifteen minutes decide the whole shift.

A scenario-based onboarding experience for last-mile delivery professionals — designed, art-directed, and built end to end.

First 15 is a blended onboarding experience designed for newly hired delivery associates and dispatch coordinators at MileOne Logistics, a realistic fictional carrier created for this project. A Rise 360 course teaches a three-part decision model; an 8–12 minute Storyline 360 branching scenario puts it under pressure. Learners triage a tire-pressure alert, a damaged label, a medical delivery window, and an unsafe access note — then see how their choices moved Safety, Service, and Time.

01The challenge

The gap isn't knowledge. It's deciding.

A last-mile delivery operation loses more service and safety ground in the first fifteen minutes of a shift than in the eight hours that follow. That window is where an associate finds the tire-pressure alert, the label they cannot read, the medical delivery with a receiving window, and the dispatch note that today's volume is heavier than planned. It is also the window where a new hire has the least context and the most pressure to simply leave.

Conventional onboarding is badly matched to this. It front-loads policy — handbooks, compliance modules, a checklist to acknowledge — and then sends a nine-day-old associate into a morning that demands judgment, prioritisation, and the confidence to interrupt a dispatcher. Most new associates can recite the pre-shift checklist. The gap is decision-making under competing pressure, which is the one thing a checklist cannot teach and a knowledge check cannot measure.

So the design problem was not "build a pre-shift course." It was:

How do you let someone practise the first fifteen minutes of a bad morning forty times before it counts?

02The learner and business context

Two people share the same fifteen minutes from opposite sides of a radio.

MileOne Logistics is a realistic fictional regional carrier: contracted parcel volume plus a growing medical-courier line, operating out of metro stations with a mixed cargo-van fleet. Like the real operations it is modelled on, it runs with high seasonal hiring, a compressed training window, and dispatch coordinators who are often promoted from the driver's seat with no training in how to coordinate.

The delivery associate is capable and motivated but overwhelmed — by the handheld, the route sequence, the safety procedure, the customer notes, and the unfamiliar question of when it is acceptable to interrupt someone. The dispatch coordinator is six weeks into a promotion, holding fourteen routes, and has learned that vague escalations cost them their whole morning.

Designing for both at once was the decision that shaped the course. The escalation criteria are not a driver policy — they are a shared contract. Associates learn what a complete escalation contains; coordinators learn to ask for the same four fields. When both sides of a radio call are trained on the same model, the model survives contact with the operation.

03My design role

Sole practitioner, end to end.

I worked this the way a contract LXD is typically hired: given a performance problem and a deadline, and expected to return a working experience.

That meant framing the business problem and writing the brief; defining two personas and the performance gap; writing measurable objectives and mapping every one to an interaction and a piece of assessment evidence; choosing the modality split and defending it; architecting the branching scenario with its variables, thresholds, and remediation logic; storyboarding to developer-handoff standard; art-directing the visual system, including a palette validated for contrast rather than chosen by eye; building in Rise 360 and Storyline 360; specifying the accessibility requirements and the test workflow; and writing the measurement plan a client would use to decide whether the thing worked.

I also used AI deliberately and narrowly — for first-pass dialogue variants, for structuring the objective-to-evidence matrix, and for scripting a repetitive QA pass across slide states. The design decisions, the instructional model, and the writing voice are mine. It's worth being precise about that, because "AI-enabled workflow" is only a credential if you can say where the judgment lived.

04Learning goals & instructional strategy

Three dispositions, never two.

The experience is designed to target four performance behaviours, not four topics: triage a pre-shift discovery within ninety seconds; apply four escalation triggers to decide whether an issue needs someone else; compose an escalation message complete enough to be acted on without a follow-up question; and sequence a route so time-sensitive and custody-controlled deliveries are protected before volume is absorbed.

Every discovery gets one of three dispositions, decided in under ninety seconds:

FIX

I can resolve this myself, inside the pre-shift window, safely, and within policy.

FLAG

Someone else has the authority, information, or resources. Escalate now, with a complete message, and keep working.

NOTE

This doesn't change today's plan, but it goes on the record.

The insight this rests on

New hires don't fail because they can't tell a big problem from a small one. They fail because they treat every problem as binary — deal with it alone or stop everything — and so they under-escalate real risk and over-escalate noise. Three dispositions beat two.

And four triggers instead of "when appropriate"

Risk
Roadworthiness, load security, or anyone's physical safety — yours, the public's, a customer's.
Custody
Cold chain, signature required, controlled goods, anything sealed or carrying a temperature indicator. Custody items don't get judgment calls applied to them.
Promise
A committed window or service level you now can't reliably make. The trigger is the risk to the promise, not the confirmed failure.
Ambiguity
Information you can't resolve with what you've got, where being wrong has a cost. An address you're 80% sure of is ambiguity.

Four strategy choices

Scenario-based practice as the assessment, not the dessert. The branching scenario isn't a reward for finishing the module; it is the assessment. Every decision point is a scored Storyline question with points assigned per choice, so the LMS receives a defensible score while the learner experiences a story.

Consequence before correction. Choose to top up the tire and go, and the coaching doesn't appear immediately — the pressure warning returns at stop nine, on a bridge approach, and then the station lead talks to you. Adults revise a mental model when the model visibly fails, not when they're told it's wrong.

Time is a budget, not a score. Safety and Service are 0–100 scores; Time is a fifteen-minute allowance that choices consume. That single modelling decision teaches the precedence order structurally, without a line of narration telling learners what to value.

Cognitive load managed by tool. Nothing is taught twice, and nothing is assessed where it was taught.

Safety is a gate, not a trade-off. Service is a promise you protect or renegotiate early. Time is the thing you spend to do both.

05Storyline 360 build highlights

A state machine wearing a story.

Twenty-five named variables track Safety, Service, and the remaining time budget, plus the learner's choice at each decision point, the count of escalations raised, a remediation flag, and a concatenated path string such as 1B-2A-3C-4A. Thirty-eight slides across seven scenes.

A few constructions a developer will recognise as the real work:

  • A hub-and-spoke shift-start dashboard with four inspectable cards. "Roll out" stays disabled until all four have a Visited state — a complete pre-shift review enforced by the interface rather than by an instruction.
  • Points assigned per choice on graded Pick One questions, so a native results slide produces a defensible LMS score while custom Safety, Service, and Time variables drive the coaching.
  • A safety floor rather than a wrong-answer trap. A categorical violation interrupts, a two-screen micro-lesson runs, and the learner re-decides with a remediation flag set — so the LMS can distinguish "got it right" from "got there on the second pass."
  • A five-layer debrief that replays the path and gives pattern-specific coaching. A learner who was fast and exposed gets different advice from one who was careful and costly, because those two people need opposite advice.

The most important build note in the project

At DP4, the "I'll decide at the gate" path must end with nothing bad happening. The dog turns out to be crated; the learner enters and is fine. A designer's instinct is to punish the deferral with a bite — that would teach luck rather than process. The point is that a good outcome from a bad process is the most dangerous feedback there is, and the station lead says so out loud.

06Rise 360 companion experience

Rise reflows. Storyline scales. That's the whole argument.

Rise carries two jobs Storyline is genuinely worse at.

Before the scenario, Prepare for the Shift teaches the model in five short lessons using blocks chosen for instructional reason: a labelled graphic over a photographed van bay for the pre-shift inspection zones, because spatial knowledge should be learned spatially; a sorting activity that forces learners to place eight real discoveries into Fix, Flag, and Note, because the model is only learned when applied to cases that don't announce their category; flashcards for the four triggers, because that is genuinely recall; and a scenario block for one short escalation conversation, as low-stakes rehearsal before the high-stakes one.

After the scenario, Field Guide and Transfer Toolkit is built to be re-opened on a phone in a van at 6:55 a.m. — which is why it's Rise and not a PDF. It holds the one-page decision guide, the escalation template, a reflection prompt, and a manager coaching guide with five questions a supervisor can ask in a four-minute ride-along.

The reading a learner does on a phone belongs in Rise. The branching state a learner generates belongs in Storyline.

07Assessment & feedback design

Name the consequence before naming the rule.

Assessment is designed to evaluate judgment, which means no item in this experience can be answered by remembering a sentence from a previous screen.

A five-item confidence check opens the scenario and repeats at the end, so the debrief can show a confidence shift alongside a performance score. Rise carries five embedded knowledge checks on the criteria themselves. The Storyline scenario carries the performance assessment: four decision points, points assigned by choice, 80% to pass.

Feedback follows one rule throughout. Not "Incorrect. Safety issues must be escalated," but:

Feedback, option B at decision point one

You made your first sixty stops. On the sixty-first, the pressure warning came back on a bridge approach, and you spent forty minutes on the shoulder waiting for roadside. A seventeen-psi overnight drop is a leak, not the weather. One reading tells you whether the van is safe now. Two readings tell you whether it will be safe at noon. You had two.

Every distractor is a real mistake a reasonable person makes under pressure, and each one's feedback addresses the specific reasoning behind it rather than restating policy.

08Experience design & visual direction

Operational software doesn't have to look like operational software.

The reference points are architectural photography, Japanese spatial restraint, and editorial fashion layout — a near-black ground, generous negative space, ivory content surfaces that behave like paper, a single burnished gold accent used structurally rather than decoratively, and one restrained indigo reserved exclusively for things the learner can act on.

That last rule does most of the work. In this system, indigo means "you can touch this." Gold means "this is a structural or evaluative element" — a rule, a meter, a scene marker. Because the interaction colour is reserved and never used for emphasis, a learner never has to hunt for what is clickable, and a keyboard user never has to guess where focus is.

The interface avoids the two clichés of the genre. There is no cartoon office and no stock-photo handshake: imagery is documentary, high-contrast, and cropped tight on the real objects of the work — a tote seal, a pressure gauge, a handheld screen, a route sequence. And there are no gradients, no glassmorphism, and no animation that exists to prove animation is possible.

Every colour pair was checked with a contrast calculator rather than an eye. Three of my first-pass tokens failed WCAG AA and were replaced.

The finding that only comes from measuring

Burnished gold on warm ivory is the most attractive pairing in the system and it is unusable for text at any size — 2.83:1. On charcoal it measures 5.66:1 and is fine. A designer working by eye would have shipped the ivory pairing, because it looks right. The other two replacements were a caution amber at 4.13:1 and a secondary grey at 4.43:1 — a near miss that would have failed an audit.

Typography

Bodoni Moda carries the editorial register, used for scene titles and never below 28px — high-contrast didone serifs lose their thin strokes at small sizes, which is a legibility problem rather than a taste one. Inter does the work: everything a learner reads to make a decision, 18px minimum. IBM Plex Mono exists because this scenario is full of numbers learners compare against each other, and comparison requires tabular alignment.

09Accessibility & inclusive design

Designed in, then tested. Not retrofitted.

The concrete consequences: no real-time timers anywhere, so the pressure is narrative and a learner using assistive technology isn't penalised for reading speed; every status communicated by an icon and a word as well as a colour, so the Safety, Service, and Time meters never depend on hue; a focus indicator built as an ivory ring with a dark inner stroke so it's visible on charcoal, ivory, and indigo alike; focus order set explicitly on every slide, with decorative art marked decorative so a screen-reader user hears the decision, not the background; captions and a downloadable transcript for all narration; and a reduced-motion preference the learner sets before the story starts.

Inclusive representation was specified, not assumed: the cast spans age, gender, body type, ethnicity, and visible assistive equipment, and no character's competence correlates with their identity. The dispatcher who handles escalations best is the newest coordinator in the building.

Removing the countdown forced the design to create pressure through consequence instead — and consequence is what teaches. The constraint improved the design.

10Measurement plan

The cheapest instrument is the one that improves the course.

The proposed plan works across four levels, mapped loosely to Kirkpatrick because that's the language most L&D stakeholders share. At the reaction level it would track completion, time in module, drop-off point, and a two-question relevance rating. At the learning level: the pre-and-post confidence shift, the performance score, and — the most useful artefact in the whole plan — the decision-path distribution.

Because the scenario records a path string for every learner, the plan would surface which specific distractor the cohort selects most often. That's a curriculum instrument, not a report: if 60% of learners top up the tire and drive, the gap is in how Lesson 3 teaches the difference between a leak and a temperature variance, and the fix is one Rise screen.

At the behaviour level it would use a manager observation checklist during the first ride-along and sample escalation messages for completeness against the four fields. At the results level it proposes watching pre-shift window duration, avoidable first-attempt failures, and preventable roadside events across the first thirty days on route.

Stated once, plainly

Every measure above is a proposed measure. This is a portfolio prototype with no live learner population — there are no completion rates here, no performance data, and no operational outcomes. In a live implementation I would validate the baseline across at least one full hiring cohort before attributing any change to the training, and I'd expect the escalation-completeness rubric to need revision after the first twenty real messages.

12What I'd test, improve, and scale

Three honest next steps.

What I'd test first. Whether the ninety-second triage claim survives real use. The framework is built around a time box, and if new associates can't actually reach a disposition in ninety seconds on a cold February morning, it needs a fourth disposition or a simpler trigger set. I'd run five think-aloud sessions with associates inside their first three weeks, at a real station, before the shift.

What I'd improve. The fourth decision point is the weakest link in the current branch. Customer access notes vary enormously by station and by contract, and one scenario can't carry that variance. In a live build I'd replace the single note with a pool of three swapped in by a random variable, so a second attempt isn't a memory test.

What I'd scale. The architecture generalises: the dashboard, the three-dimension scoring, the remediation pattern, and the debrief are a reusable shell. Swapping the content layer produces a first-fifteen-minutes experience for a warehouse shift start, a clinic opening, or a field-service morning, at a fraction of the original build cost. That reusability is the thing I'd actually pitch to a director of L&D: not this course, but the pattern and the production system behind it.

13Final reflection

The model was the hard part. The tooling wasn't.

My first version of the decision framework had two dispositions — handle it or escalate it — and it produced a scenario where every correct answer was "escalate," which is both useless as training and untrue to the job. Adding "Note" as a third disposition fixed the instruction, the branching, and the scoring in one move, because it created a middle path where most real pre-shift discoveries actually live.

The second thing: designing the accessibility requirements first made the experience better for everyone. Removing the real-time timer — which I'd added early because pressure felt authentic — forced me to create pressure through consequence instead. And consequence is what teaches.

What I'd want a hiring manager to take from this project is not that I can operate Storyline. It's that I can be handed a messy operational problem, find the actual performance gap inside it, build a model simple enough to fit on a wallet card, and then produce something learners will finish and managers will reinforce — and that I'll tell you plainly which parts of it still need to be tested.

Accessibility

Designed to WCAG 2.1 AA, with accessibility decided at the storyboard rather than audited at the end. Contrast ratios were calculated for every colour pair — three first-pass tokens failed and were replaced. Every interaction is keyboard-operable with a visible dual-tone focus indicator. No information is conveyed by colour alone. Narration is captioned and available as a transcript, and the module is complete with the sound off. There are no timed interactions anywhere. Tested keyboard-only, with NVDA on Chrome and VoiceOver on iOS Safari, at 200% text zoom, and fully muted.
